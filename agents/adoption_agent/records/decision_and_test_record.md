## Independent judgment

I recommend a **six-week feasibility proof of concept**, capped at the hypothetical $30,000 learning budget, for a midsize food-and-beverage manufacturer considering electrically heated refractory-brick thermal storage for process steam. Management should investigate whether flexible charging can meet its heat demand economically before considering a hardware pilot. It should retain the gas boiler and make no installation commitment now.

The decisive factor is the gap between reported industrial operation and an executable local business case. A thermal battery can be technically plausible while the plant lacks affordable charging hours, electrical capacity or suitable steam integration. The strongest reason to reverse my learning recommendation would be credible early evidence that connection costs or delivered-heat economics are dominated by a simpler alternative. A favorable study would justify a proposal for a bounded experiment, not automatic production adoption.

The company, budget, schedule and thresholds are explicit teaching assumptions. No real plant data, vendor quote, physical pilot, purchase or emissions reduction is claimed.

## Three levels of finding

**ET generally.** Electric thermal batteries have reported industrial steam operation [E2]. Established heaters and refractory materials do not by themselves establish the reliability and economics of every integrated installation. The source set establishes commercialization activity; it does not establish majority diffusion, independent lifetime performance or a universal price advantage.

**For process steam.** Charging at different times from steam delivery may fit industrial heat demand, but temperature, pressure, quality, duty cycle and backup must be matched. Compare demand reduction, heat recovery, suitable heat pumps and direct electric boilers before paying for storage. High storage efficiency does not establish low delivered-heat cost.

**For this organization.** The fast follower has engineering resources and permits reversible learning. Missing local data block installation but can be investigated in a nonproduction POC. Under the conservative contrast policy, the same POC is deferred pending independently reviewable comparable operating evidence. Technology facts stay fixed.

## Recommendation categories and decision logic

| Category | Meaning and release condition |
|---|---|
| ADOPT_IMPLEMENT | Propose controlled production only after exact-application engineering, performance, reliability, economics, support and governance gates pass; human approval is required. |
| BOUNDED_EXPERIMENT | Capped comparative trial with baseline, owner, metrics, stop rules and a reversible boundary. |
| PROOF_OF_CONCEPT | Resolve feasibility before a comparative pilot or purchase; may be a desktop study, explicitly identified as such. |
| MONITOR | Gather defined external signals with an owner and review date; no active implementation. |
| DEFER | A relevant application is blocked by a named readiness, evidence, authority or commercial gate. |
| REJECT_FOR_PRESENT | Credible evidence shows poor fit or dominated value within the horizon; specify reopening conditions. |

Decision order: establish the business need; assess evidence and alternatives; apply consequence-related gates; then choose the least commitment that answers the management question. Missing evidence cannot support installation, but it can support a bounded learning task. Posture affects acceptable uncertainty and timing; it cannot create evidence or waive engineering authority.

## Bounded proof of concept

**Owner and scope.** Energy manager leads with plant engineering, finance and quality assurance; external consultant support may be used within the assumed cap. Six weeks, maximum $30,000, review December 14, 2026. No production changes or hardware purchase.

**Weeks 1 and 2.** Obtain historical interval heat and fuel records, peak and seasonal conditions, steam specifications, available charging windows and preliminary utility constraints. New metering may supplement records but a short sample cannot establish seasonality.

**Weeks 3 and 4.** Model useful heat delivery and charging capacity. Compare alternatives using consistent boundaries, losses, auxiliaries and backup. Assess cost and emissions under multiple electricity/fuel conditions.

**Weeks 5 and 6.** Seek scoped installed and service terms from candidate suppliers, utility connection/tariff terms and independent operating references. Produce a comparison and a proposed pilot scope if warranted.

**Go gate.** Propose a separate bounded experiment only if engineering can specify compatible steam and backup, utility terms permit the duty cycle, and conservative full-cost scenarios show a credible route to the board-approved investment hurdle. Obtain that hurdle from management; no universal payback target is invented. A later experiment should measure electricity input, useful steam output, parasitic loads, availability, restart/backup behavior and cost against the incumbent. Engineering sets a safe test boundary and stop criteria before live testing.

**Stop or defer gate.** Stop before exceeding the cap or altering live equipment. Defer capital if necessary data or commercial terms cannot be obtained. Reassess the technology choice if a simpler alternative provides better value. A successful model or short trial cannot certify lifetime reliability.

## Economic sensitivity illustration

The following inputs are hypothetical arithmetic examples, not market prices, vendor guarantees or plant findings. Let the complete electricity-to-useful-heat efficiency be 0.90 and the gas-boiler useful efficiency 0.85. Assume gas costs $30 per MWh of fuel input.

Energy-only gas heat cost = 30 / 0.85 = **$35.29 per MWh of useful heat**.

| Hypothetical charging energy price | Electricity / 0.90 | Comparison with gas energy only |
|---|---|---|
| $20 per MWh electrical | $22.22 per MWh useful heat | $13.07 lower |
| $50 per MWh electrical | $55.56 per MWh useful heat | $20.26 higher |

The energy-only break-even charging price is 30 × 0.90 / 0.85 = **$31.76 per MWh electrical**. This excludes capital, finance, demand/network charges, connection upgrades, maintenance and backup differences. Those costs can erase an apparent energy advantage. This is why efficient storage alone cannot justify adoption. Full economics must use actual executable terms, not wholesale-price headlines. No net emissions claim follows without a defined electricity and fuel emissions boundary.
